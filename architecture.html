01

The brief, restated

Eight industries, seven pest classes, thirteen analytics, three output surfaces. Underneath all of it sits one engineering problem worth naming precisely, because everything else follows from it.

Almost nothing happens, and when it does it is small, fast, and dark. A warehouse camera sees roughly 650,000 frames in a twelve-hour night. Perhaps twenty of them contain a rat. That rat is 25 cm long, moving at 1.5 m/s, lit only by an infrared illuminator, and it is gone in four seconds. The system's entire job is to find those twenty frames without drowning the client in the other 649,980.

That framing produces three non-negotiable design consequences, and they shape every layer below:

Consequence 1

Compute must be gated

Running a detector on every frame of every camera is 26× more GPU than the job needs. Cheap motion gating first, expensive inference only on candidate regions. This is what makes 16 cameras per edge box affordable.

Consequence 2

Never alert on one frame

A single-frame detection is a coin flip. A track — the same object, classified consistently, moving plausibly across a dozen frames — is evidence. All alerting happens at track level, never detection level.

Consequence 3

Pixels on target rule everything

No model recovers a rat rendered at 14 pixels. Camera placement, lens choice and shutter speed determine the ceiling on accuracy before a single line of ML code is written. Site survey is phase zero.

What "passive" buys, and what it costs

Passive means the software observes feeds without controlling cameras, without lures, and without interrupting operations. That is the right commercial posture — it deploys into a live food plant on a Tuesday without a shutdown — but it means the system inherits whatever the existing CCTV was built for. Security cameras are mounted high and angled at human torso height with a wide field of view. Pests live at floor level, hug wall–floor junctions, and are one-tenth the size. Passive analysis of a security-optimised camera estate will underperform, and the survey in §2 is how we find out by how much before committing.

Assumption stated up front

This architecture assumes ExpressVision is a service Express Pesticides sells to its clients — multi-tenant, many sites per tenant, technicians and client staff as separate roles — rather than an internal-only tool. Eight named industries points strongly that way. If it is internal-only, the tenancy layer, per-client branding and client-facing reports collapse out and roughly six weeks come off the roadmap. Flagged again in §16.

02

Camera physics: the accuracy ceiling

Two numbers set the upper bound on what any model can achieve at a site. Both are fixed by hardware, not software, and both are usually wrong on an inherited estate.

Pixels on target

Detection needs roughly 40 px across the animal's long axis; reliable species identification needs about 80 px. Working backwards from a 25 cm rodent gives a maximum useful range per camera configuration:

Camera configuration Lens / HFOV Detect (40 px) Identify (80 px) Typical role
2 MP (1920 px wide)2.8 mm · ~100°5.0 m2.5 mExisting wide-area security cam — marginal
2 MP6 mm · ~53°12.0 m6.0 mCorridor / aisle run
4 MP (2560 px wide)4 mm · ~78°9.9 m4.9 mGeneral-purpose pest-view
4 MP8 mm · ~40°22.0 m11.0 mLong aisle, dock approach
8 MP / 4K4 mm · ~78°14.8 m7.4 mWide coverage where bandwidth allows

Ranges assume a 25 cm target spanning the horizontal axis and no optical degradation. Treat as planning figures for the survey, then verify with a tape measure and a test target on site.

Shutter speed — the failure nobody looks for

A rat travelling 1.5 m/s covers 5 cm in a 1/30 s exposure. At the pixel density that puts 40 px on the animal, that is a 9-pixel smear across a 40-pixel target — enough to destroy the shape the detector is looking for. Most installed cameras drop their shutter to 1/8 s or slower at night to preserve image brightness, which turns every moving pest into a streak.

Hard requirement

Every camera in scope must be configurable to a minimum shutter of 1/250 s in night mode, which in turn requires enough IR illumination at the target distance to expose correctly. This single setting will do more for detection accuracy than any model improvement, and it costs nothing but a config push and, sometimes, a supplementary illuminator.

The survey deliverable

Phase 0 produces, per camera: resolution and codec, lens focal length, mounted height and tilt, measured distance to the nearest and furthest floor point in view, night shutter and gain behaviour, IR illuminator reach, and a five-minute overnight sample clip. From that we mark each camera usable, usable after reconfiguration, or needs supplementary camera, and produce a bill of materials.

Supplementary pest-view cameras, where needed, are specified deliberately against the physics above: mounted 0.3–1.2 m high, aimed along a wall–floor junction rather than across a room, 4 MP with a moderate lens, fixed shutter floor, and dedicated IR. One well-placed pest-view camera on a loading dock threshold outperforms six ceiling domes.

03

System layers

Six layers, with the boundary that matters drawn at the site uplink. Everything needed to detect, validate, record and alert lives on-site; the cloud aggregates, analyses and presents. A site that loses its internet link keeps working and backfills later.

LayerResponsibilityRuns whereNotes that matter
L0 · CaptureVideo and sensor acquisitionSitePull the main stream, not the sub-stream — sub-streams are D1 and destroy small targets. Gate on a downscaled copy instead.
L1 · Edge inferenceGate, detect, track, validate, assemble events, bufferSiteFully autonomous. Holds 120 s rolling video per camera so evidence clips start before the detection.
L2 · TransportEvent and media deliveryBothMQTT/TLS for metadata, presigned resumable PUT for media, bandwidth-capped so it never saturates a site link during business hours.
L3 · Core servicesStorage, rules, analytics, reportingCloud or on-prem serverSame container set either way — hospitals and some food clients will require on-prem, so this must not assume cloud.
L4 · ApplicationsConsole, mobile, alert channels, APIClient-facingThe verify control in the event feed is also the training-data pipeline. See §11.
L5 · MLOpsLabelling, training, registry, rolloutInternalNot an afterthought. The system's accuracy comes from this loop turning, not from the initial model.

Bandwidth budget

An event carries about 2 KB of metadata and a 15-second H.264 clip at roughly 4 MB. A busy site producing 200 events a night moves about 800 MB overnight — trivial for a fibre link, manageable on 4G with the throttle set. Metadata alone is under half a megabyte a night, so a site on a degraded link can be configured to send events immediately and defer clips to a daily window without losing analytics fidelity.

04

The gated cascade

Seven stages, each of which exists to throw work away. The compounding discard rate is the central performance property of the system — it is what turns an intractable 24/7 inference problem into something a $600 edge box handles for sixteen cameras.

Stage by stage

A

Decode and downscale

Hardware decode via NVDEC. The full-resolution frame stays resident in GPU memory; a 480p greyscale copy goes to the gate. Never gate on a downscaled frame and then infer on it too — the whole point is keeping native pixels available for stage C.

B

Motion gate

MOG2 background subtraction with per-camera adaptive learning rate and shadow suppression, morphological open/close, contour extraction to candidate ROI boxes. Rejects on implausible area and aspect ratio.

Two guards that field deployments always end up needing: a global-change guard that detects a light switch, an IR-cut filter toggle at dawn, or a bumped camera (>25% of pixels changed) and resets the background model instead of emitting thousands of candidates; and tamper/defocus detection via Laplacian variance collapse, which catches the spider web across the lens that silently kills more outdoor analytics than any other cause.

discards ~96% of frames
C

Tiled detection at native resolution

Each ROI is expanded to a 640×640 tile cut from the full-resolution frame, tiles are batched across all cameras on the node, and a TensorRT INT8 detector runs the batch. Boxes are mapped back to full-frame coordinates and NMS is applied there, so objects straddling tile edges resolve correctly.

This is the single most consequential decision in the vision pipeline — Figure 3 shows why.

~1.5 tiles per gated frame
D

Multi-object tracking

ByteTrack with a constant-velocity Kalman filter, per camera. ByteTrack is chosen specifically because it associates low-confidence detections into existing tracks — exactly the regime pest detection lives in, where a rat behind a pallet leg drops to 0.3 confidence for six frames. Track buffer of ~30 frames survives occlusion behind racking.

E

Track validation — the false-positive firewall

A track is promoted to an event only if it clears every gate: at least 5 detections, a consistent top class across at least 3 frames, net displacement above a threshold, and a motion-plausibility score.

Plausibility is a small gradient-boosted classifier over track features — speed profile, acceleration bounds, net-to-gross displacement ratio, scale stability, body-axis-to-heading alignment. It separates living creatures from the things that fool frame-level detectors: swaying vegetation oscillates about a fixed point so net displacement is near zero; a wind-blown bag tumbles with implausible acceleration; a water drop or near-lens insect shows extreme apparent speed with out-of-plane scale change. It is cheap, and it is trained on the operator verdicts from §11.

discards ~93% of tracks
F

Event assembly

Pulls 8 s pre-roll and 5 s post-roll from the ring buffer, cuts the clip, renders an annotated keyframe, simplifies the trajectory (Ramer–Douglas–Peucker), projects it to floor-plane coordinates through the camera homography, assigns zones, and computes dwell time, heading and speed.

G

Cross-camera and temporal association

Events at the same site, on adjacent cameras, within a short window and in adjacent zones merge into one incident. Without this, one rat crossing three overlapping camera views is counted three times and every analytic downstream is inflated.

05

A taxonomy that degrades gracefully

The requested species list mixes classes a detector can separate reliably with pairs it cannot — a rat and a mouse at twelve metres in infrared are the same grey blob. Forcing a fine-grained answer the pixels do not support is how these systems lose client trust.

The system therefore classifies hierarchically and reports at the deepest level the evidence justifies:

LevelClassesEmitted whenConfidence in practice
L1 · Presenceanimal human vehicle other-motionAlwaysHigh — this is where recall is protected
L2 · Grouprodent bird reptile carnivore insectAlways, with a confidence scoreGood above ~40 px on target
L3 · Speciesrat/mouse, gecko/snake, dog/cat, bird typeOnly when box ≥ 80 px and L3 confidence clears thresholdVariable — suppressed rather than guessed

The UI shows rodent — rat likely (0.62) rather than a confident mouse. Analytics roll up at L2 by default so that trend lines are not corrupted by L3 noise, with L3 breakdowns available where the sample supports them. The client's optional "pest species distribution" requirement is satisfied at L2 immediately and at L3 as the training set matures.

Why human detection is mandatory

People are not pests, but the class earns its place four times over: it suppresses false alerts from human shadows and reflections, it contextualises activity (movement during staffed hours means something different), it drives privacy blurring before any clip leaves the site, and it verifies service visits — proof that a technician actually walked the dock at 02:40 is a commercial product in its own right for a pest-control company.

One addition worth making

The brief leaves room under "other relevant animals/insects", and cockroaches belong there. They are the dominant complaint in food processing, restaurants and hotels — three of the eight named industries — and they are technically tractable: nocturnal, high-contrast against floors and walls under IR, 2–4 cm, moving in-plane on flat surfaces. They need close-range cameras (under about 2.5 m, so kitchen and prep-area placements) but within that constraint they detect well, and the glue-board technique in §6 counts them precisely. Recommend adding as an L2 class in Phase 2.

06

Flying insects, honestly

Of everything in the brief, this is the requirement most likely to disappoint if approached literally. It is worth being straight about why, and then redirecting to the version that genuinely works — which happens to be closer to how pest control already operates.

Detecting and identifying individual flying insects in open air is not reliably solvable with room cameras. Near the lens an insect is an enormous, motion-blurred, out-of-focus smear with no stable appearance; a few metres away it is sub-pixel. In between it is a fast-moving two-pixel dot with no texture for a classifier to work with. There is no camera placement that puts a housefly in the 40-pixel sweet spot across a useful volume of a room.

So the system splits the requirement into two things it can actually deliver:

Track 1 · ambient

Flying Insect Activity Index

Do not name individuals — measure the phenomenon. Count qualifying motion-blob events per minute in a calibrated volume, normalise per camera and per illumination condition, and publish a trend, not a count. Correlated against temperature and humidity (§8) this answers the question IPM actually asks: is flying insect pressure rising in this zone this week?

Delivered as an index with a baseline band, never as "14 flies detected", because that number would be wrong and the client would eventually discover it.

Track 2 · instrumented

Glue-board and bait-station counting

Point a fixed, close-range, evenly-lit camera at an insect light trap glue board and run a small-object detector on a still frame every 30 minutes. The insects are stationary, in-plane, high-contrast, at known scale and known distance — every property that made the open-air problem hard is now solved by the mounting.

Counting becomes accurate, species grouping becomes feasible, and the output is already the KPI the industry uses. The same approach instruments bait stations for rodent take and visit counts.

Track 2 is the recommendation to lead with commercially. It converts an unreliable computer-vision problem into a reliable one purely through fixturing, it automates a task technicians currently do by hand with a clicker on a monthly walk, and it produces defensible numbers for food-safety audits. It also builds the labelled insect dataset that eventually improves Track 1.

Set this expectation in writing

The contract and the demo should both present flying insects as an activity index plus instrumented counting, not as per-insect detection and identification in open air. Agreeing this before build is far cheaper than renegotiating it at acceptance testing.

07

Analytics engine

Every analytic in the brief, mapped to the data it comes from and the method that computes it. Two ideas do disproportionate work here: zones, which make numbers actionable, and uptime normalisation, which stops the system from quietly lying.

Zones and the floor plane

During commissioning an operator draws polygons on each camera view — dock threshold, dry store aisle 3, waste bay, prep counter, wall junction east — and marks each frame edge as either a physical boundary or a continuation of open space. Optionally they click four ground-plane correspondence points to establish a homography onto the site floor plan.

Zones turn "17 detections on camera 4" into "17 rodent events at the dock threshold", which a technician can act on. The homography turns per-camera heat maps into a single site-wide heat map on the actual floor plan, which is what a client wants to see in a monthly review.

The way these systems lie

A camera that was offline for three nights produces zero events, and an un-normalised dashboard renders that as zero pest activity — the most dangerous possible false reassurance in a food plant. Every count in the system is divided by observed camera-hours, and any report covering a period with less than 90% coverage carries an explicit coverage figure on its face.

Inferring entry points from where tracks begin

The brief asks for entry-point identification, and there is an elegant way to get it that needs no extra hardware. A pest entering through a gap appears in frame for the first time at that gap. Individually a track origin means little; across weeks, the origins pile up.

From findings to recommendations

The brief's final report requirement — "recommendations for inspection" — is served by a rules engine over the analytics above, not by a language model inventing advice. Findings map to actions through an IPM rule table maintained with Express Pesticides' own technicians, so the output carries their expertise rather than generic guidance:

RULE FIRED · SITE 041 · WEEK 32
rodent origin-cluster persistent ≥ 7 nights
  AND zone.type = dock
  AND peak_hour ∈ [00:00, 04:00]
→ inspect dock-door seal and brush strip at cluster A
→ place snap traps along east wall junction, 3 m spacing
→ re-survey after 14 nights; expect origin count < 8

Every recommendation carries the evidence that triggered it — clip, trajectory, cluster statistics — so a technician can judge it rather than take it on faith, and so the client can see why they are being asked to spend money.

IoT sensing and correlation

The brief is explicit that IoT is required but should be applied where it earns its place. The value is not in the readings themselves — it is in what they explain about the video, and in the one sensor class that turns detection into diagnosis.

SensorWhereWhat it explainsPriority
Temperature + humidityPer zone, 1 per 400 m²Rodent and insect activity are strongly temperature-driven; supplies the covariate that keeps period comparisons honestCore
Door / shutter contactEvery dock door and external doorTies entry events to operational behaviour — the single highest-value correlation in the systemCore
Ambient lightPer camera groupNormalises the insect activity index and explains detector confidence shifts at dusk/dawnPhase 3
Bait station triggerPer stationIR beam or vibration; gives take/visit data where a camera is impracticalPhase 3
ILT glue-board cameraPer trapAccurate insect counts (§6)Phase 3
Differential pressureDock and clean-room thresholdsNegative pressure draws insects inward; explains persistent ingress at a threshold that looks sealedOptional

Transport. LoRaWAN for the sensor fleet, with a single gateway per site feeding MQTT into the edge node. The reasoning is specific to the environment: warehouses and factories are RF-hostile — steel racking, concrete, metal-clad walls — and LoRa penetrates where Wi-Fi and Zigbee will not, at battery lives measured in years rather than months. Small sites with good Wi-Fi coverage can use ESP32-class nodes instead and skip the gateway.

What correlation actually produces

Readings land in the same time-series store as events, which makes the analysis straightforward: bin nights by mean overnight temperature in 2°C steps, compute event rate per bin, and test for a lagged relationship — activity often follows a temperature drop by one to three days as pests move indoors. Door-contact correlation is even more direct: window each dock-open event and measure event rate inside it against the site baseline.

The outputs read like this, and they are what makes the product worth paying for:

2.4×Rodent event rate when overnight minimum falls below 18 °C, site 041, 62 nights observed
83%Of dock-zone rodent events begin within 20 minutes of a dock-door open event

The second finding is the kind of thing that changes client behaviour: it converts "you have rats" into "your rats arrive with the 22:15 delivery, and the door stands open for eleven minutes".

Statistical discipline

Correlations are surfaced as associations, never as causes, and only once a minimum sample is met — at least 30 observed nights and 20 events, with the confidence interval shown. A dashboard that reports a spurious correlation from four nights of data will be believed, acted on, and then disbelieved permanently.

09

Alerting without fatigue

The brief asks for three alert types. The engineering difficulty is not producing them — it is producing few enough of them that someone still reads the fourth one.

Type 1

Real-time detection

Fires on a validated high-confidence event in a zone configured for immediate notification. Carries the annotated keyframe inline so the recipient decides in two seconds.

Type 2

Repeated activity

Fires when the repeat-visit cluster from §7 crosses a threshold — same zone, same species, consistent hour, N nights. This is the alert that drives inspections.

Type 3

High activity

Fires when a zone's uptime-normalised rate exceeds its own rolling baseline by a z-score threshold. Site-relative, so a naturally busy waste bay does not alert nightly.

The five mechanisms that keep the volume sane

  • Confidence tiering. Only high-confidence events push. Medium-confidence events appear in the review feed without notifying anyone. Nothing uncertain ever interrupts a person — it waits to be looked at.
  • Cooldown. Per camera and species, typically 15 minutes. One rat crossing a dock six times is one alert, not six.
  • Digest rollover. Past a threshold of alerts per hour, the channel switches automatically to an hourly digest and says so. A genuine infestation should not produce a hundred notifications.
  • Routing and quiet hours. Per site, per role, per zone. The night supervisor gets dock alerts at 03:00; the account manager gets the morning summary.
  • Escalation. An unacknowledged critical alert escalates — push, then SMS, then a call to the site contact after 30 minutes. Acknowledgement is one tap and is recorded against the event.

Channels. WhatsApp Business API as the primary channel for Indian deployments, with SMS fallback, email for digests and reports, and web/mobile push for logged-in users. Alert payloads carry a deep link into the event so the recipient lands on the clip, not on a dashboard home page.

Design target

Fewer than 0.5 false alerts per camera-night at the operating point. On a 16-camera site that is eight false alerts a night at the ceiling, which is the outer edge of what a client will tolerate before they mute the channel. This number, not raw model precision, is the metric to run the project against.

10

Evidence, privacy and retention

The evidence output is not just a convenience feature — in food processing it becomes an audit artefact, and in hospitals and homes it becomes a regulated one. Both pull in the same direction: capture it properly, prove it wasn't altered, and blur the people.

What an evidence package contains

  • Clip: 8 s pre-roll + track duration + 5 s post-roll, H.264, from the ring buffer
  • Annotated keyframe at peak detection confidence
  • Trajectory polyline in image and floor-plane coordinates
  • Site, camera, zone, timestamp (UTC + site-local), species with confidence, model version
  • SHA-256 content hash computed at creation on the edge node, stored in the event record

Overlays are stored as sidecar JSON and rendered on demand rather than burned into the video, with the clean original retained. A burned-in box is an assertion the client cannot independently check; a clean clip plus a separately hashed overlay is evidence they can. Object storage runs with versioning and object-lock so a clip cannot be silently replaced, which is what makes the package defensible in an FSSAI, BRC or AIB audit.

Privacy

Three of the eight target industries put the system in front of patients, residents and diners. The controls are not optional:

  • Face blur by default on any clip leaving the edge node, driven by the human class from §5; full-body blur configurable per site for hospital and residential deployments.
  • Retention defaulting to 90 days for media and 24 months for metadata, per-site overridable, enforced by lifecycle policy rather than by a cron job someone can forget to fix.
  • Role-based access with an audit log recording who viewed which clip and when — hospitals will ask for this, and it is far cheaper to build in than to retrofit.
  • DPDP Act 2023 alignment: purpose limitation, notice and signage obligations at monitored premises, defined data-fiduciary responsibilities, and a documented deletion path. Worth a short legal review before the first hospital deployment rather than after.
  • On-premises deployment option for clients whose policy forbids video leaving the building — the same container set, no cloud dependency.
11

Model strategy and the learning loop

This is the highest-risk part of the programme, because on day one there is no training data. The plan turns the pilot itself into the data factory, and makes one UI control — the verify button — the mechanism by which the system gets better.

Solving the cold start

  1. Bootstrap (weeks 0–4). MegaDetector v6 as a class-agnostic animal proposal generator — trained on camera-trap imagery, handles infrared, and generalises far better than a COCO model on this domain. It emits only animal / person / vehicle and cannot tell a rat from a cat; its job is to turn "all motion" into "mostly animals" so the labelling queue is several times denser. Supplement with public data: LILA BC camera-trap collections, iNaturalist, COCO for dog/cat/bird, and public rodent sets. The result is a v0 that is not production-grade and is not meant to be.
  2. What does not exist, and is worth not searching for twice. There is no public pretrained rodent detector worth building on. The Roboflow Universe rat and pest sets are a few hundred images each, mostly daylight or clip-art — useful later as supplementary training data, not usable as a model. The academic rodent-YOLO work (Rat-YOLO, Cosevare, open-field behaviour trackers) is trained on laboratory arenas: overhead camera, white floor, single animal, controlled lighting, and weights largely unpublished. None of it transfers to a warehouse at 2 a.m.
  3. Harvest (weeks 2–12). Deploy v0 at pilot sites in collect-only mode with a deliberately low threshold. It over-triggers on purpose, nothing is sent to a client, and every candidate goes into the labelling queue. The pilot's real deliverable is the dataset.
  4. Label. CVAT or Label Studio, two annotators, 5% overlap sample for agreement measurement. Target 8–15k labelled instances per major class for a solid v1 — rodents will and should dominate.
  5. Active learning. Prioritise the labelling queue by model uncertainty, disagreement between successive model versions, and above all by operator-marked false positives, which are the highest-information examples in the system.
  6. Per-site calibration. After roughly two weeks of verified events, each site gets its own confidence thresholds fitted to hit a target precision. Sites differ enormously in lighting, clutter and camera quality; a single global threshold is wrong nearly everywhere.

Licensing — a deployment boundary, not just a model choice

The Ultralytics YOLO family (YOLOv8/YOLO11) is AGPL-3.0. Shipping it inside a commercial product sold to clients triggers the network-copyleft obligation unless an Ultralytics Enterprise licence is purchased. The alternatives are genuinely good: RT-DETRv2 and D-FINE (Apache-2.0) are transformer detectors with comparable or better small-object accuracy, and YOLOX (Apache-2.0) is a proven permissive convolutional option.

The bootstrap tooling carries the same constraint, and this is the part that is easy to miss: PytorchWildlife depends on ultralytics and yolov5, both AGPL-3.0, whichever MegaDetector weights are selected. Choosing a permissively-licensed checkpoint does not make the installed stack permissive.

That is workable, because it defines a boundary rather than a blocker. Running MegaDetector on our own machines to sort our own collected footage is internal use — copyleft triggers on distribution and on network interaction with third parties, and a labelling pipeline is neither. What must never happen is that stack reaching an edge node at a client site. The production detector is a separately trained model exported to ONNX and served through ONNX Runtime or TensorRT, with none of it in the image. Because stage C sits behind a detector interface, this is a packaging rule rather than a code change — but it has to be written down before someone builds the edge image.

Evaluation that reflects the job

Standard mAP is necessary but not sufficient — it says nothing about whether a client will keep the system switched on. Three operational metrics govern release:

< 0.5False alerts per camera-night at the operating point
≥ 85%Recall on rodents in the per-site held-out night set
≥ 90%Detection rate on staged validation runs

Staged validation matters because recall on a labelled test set only measures what was labelled. Placing a known attractant in a known location and confirming the system reports it — alongside correlation with bait-take data from the technicians' own rounds — measures what was missed. Every site gets a staged run at commissioning and quarterly thereafter.

Postgres with TimescaleDB carries events, incidents, tracks and sensor readings as hypertables partitioned by time; continuous aggregates precompute the hourly and daily rollups that dashboards and reports read, so a monthly heat map is a cheap query rather than a scan of a hundred million rows. Media stays in object storage keyed from the Clip row — never in the database.

PHASE 18–10 weeks

Open questions

Seven answers that would sharpen this architecture. Four of them change real decisions; the architecture proceeds on the stated assumption in the meantime.

Changes the architecture

  1. Is ExpressVision sold to Express Pesticides' clients, or used internally by Express? Assumed multi-tenant SaaS throughout. If internal-only, the tenancy layer, client branding and client-facing report surface come out and roughly six weeks come off Phase 4.
  2. Will any client require on-premises deployment with no video leaving the building? Assumed yes for at least hospitals and some food processors, which is why L3 is containerised with no managed-cloud dependency. If never required, the server side simplifies and managed services become available.
  3. What is the budget for supplementary cameras per site? This determines whether Phase 0 findings lead to remediation or to reduced coverage expectations, and it is the difference between a system that works and one that is blamed for missing what it could never see.
  4. Pilot scale — how many sites and cameras, across which industries? Drives edge hardware procurement and, more importantly, how fast the training set accumulates. Two industries at three sites is the minimum useful spread.

Useful to know early

  1. Existing NVR makes and models, and whether RTSP/ONVIF access is enabled. Some consumer and cloud-locked NVRs do not expose usable streams, which turns a software integration into a hardware replacement.
  2. Is there an existing pest-control CRM or service-scheduling system to integrate with? Determines whether recommendations flow into real work orders in Phase 4 or stay in a PDF.
  3. Which sites already run insect light traps or bait stations on a documented schedule? Those are the fastest path to the instrumented counting in §6 and to validation data from technicians' existing rounds.

Recommended next step

Commission Phase 0 on two contrasting sites — ideally one food-processing or warehouse site and one hospitality or hospital site. Four weeks and a small budget resolve the largest technical unknown in the programme, produce a camera specification that everything downstream depends on, and generate the first overnight footage that the training pipeline needs. Every other decision in this document is cheaper to make afterwards.
