01

What it does

Buildings already have security cameras recording all night. Rats and mice come out at night. Nobody watches the footage, so nobody finds out until the problem is large.

ExpressVision watches the footage instead. It runs alongside the cameras that are already there, spots pests, follows them, saves proof, and tells someone.

It does four things:

Watches

Every camera, every night, without getting tired.

Recognises

Tells a rat from a cat from a plastic bag blowing past.

Records

Saves a video clip and a photo as proof, with the time and place.

Reports

Where they come in, when they are active, whether it is getting worse.

It is passive. It does not control the cameras, does not use bait or traps, and does not interrupt anything. It can be installed in a working food plant on a Tuesday without shutting anything down.

02

System architecture — what runs where

The system has two halves. One sits inside the building. One sits on a server, either in the cloud or in the client's own server room.

The split matters: everything needed to catch a pest is in the building. The server only collects, analyses and presents. If the internet goes down, the site keeps working.

How data moves

This is the same journey, but following the data rather than the boxes. At each step the data changes shape and gets much smaller.

The numbers are for one camera over one night. They are the whole point: the system's job is to get from 650,000 down to 3 without losing the rat.

Who does what, when

The software is only half the system. The other half is people. This is how a single rat sighting travels from a camera at 2 a.m. to a sealed gap in a wall.

05

The seven steps in detail

Each step exists to throw away work so the next step is affordable. That is the whole trick.

1

Read the video

Connect to the camera and turn its stream into individual pictures. We always take the camera's best-quality stream, never the low-quality backup one.

The backup stream is about a quarter the size. A rat in it is roughly a dozen dots across — too small for anything to recognise, ever.

2

Check whether anything moved

Compare each picture with what the scene normally looks like. If nothing changed, throw it away immediately.

This also has to be clever about two things: someone switching the lights on (everything changes at once — not a pest), and a spider building a web over the lens (the picture goes soft — that raises a maintenance warning, because a blind camera looks exactly like a camera with no pests).

3

Cut out the moving bit at full quality

Take a small square from the full-size picture around whatever moved, without shrinking it.

The usual approach shrinks the whole picture to a standard small size. That is fine for people and cars, and fatal for rats — a rat that was 40 dots across becomes 13, and nothing can work with that.

4

Ask what it is

The recogniser looks at the cut-out square and says what it thinks: rat, mouse, bird, cat, dog, lizard, snake, insect, person — or nothing interesting.

It only answers as precisely as the picture allows. If the animal is small and blurry it says "a rodent" rather than guessing "a rat", because a confident wrong answer costs more trust than an honest vague one.

5

Follow it across pictures

Join up the same animal from one picture to the next, building a trail.

This is harder than it sounds for a rat. It moves further than its own body length between pictures, so its position in one picture and the next do not overlap at all — the system has to predict where it went.

6

Decide whether it was real

Look at the whole trail and ask: did this thing actually cover ground? Did it move the way a living creature moves?

A plant swaying in a draught moves constantly but stays in one spot. A rat crosses the room. This single test removes about 93% of the false alarms that would otherwise reach someone.

7

Save the proof and raise the alarm

Cut a video clip from the rolling memory, save a photo with the animal marked, record the time, camera, and the path it walked. Then apply the alert rules and notify whoever should know.

If the same animal was seen by three overlapping cameras, it is merged into one sighting first — otherwise every count in every report would be inflated.

07

What the cameras need to be

This decides how well everything else can possibly work, and it is set by hardware, not software. No amount of clever code invents detail the camera never captured.

Big enough

A rat needs to be at least 40 dots across before anything can spot it, and 80 dots before we can say confidently which species it is. How far away that is depends on the camera:

CameraLensCan spot a rat up toCan identify it up to
2 MPwide (2.8 mm)5 m2.5 m
2 MPmedium (6 mm)12 m6 m
4 MPwide (4 mm)10 m5 m
4 MPnarrow (8 mm)22 m11 m
8 MP / 4Kwide (4 mm)15 m7.5 m

Fast enough

Cameras take a fraction of a second to capture each picture. At night, most cameras slow this down to let more light in — often to an eighth of a second. In that time a running rat travels several centimetres, so it comes out as a streak instead of a shape.

This is the most common reason pest detection fails on existing cameras, and it usually costs nothing to fix. The camera needs to be set to no slower than 1/250th of a second at night. That normally means adding a bit more infrared light so the picture stays bright enough — a setting change and sometimes a small lamp.

Pointed at the right thing

Security cameras are mounted high and aimed at people's faces. Pests are on the floor, hugging walls. So some sites will need a few extra low-mounted cameras looking along wall-floor junctions, doorways, drains and around bait stations.

We check all of this on the first footage from a site, before promising anything. Some cameras will turn out to be unusable, and saying so in week one is far cheaper than in month six.

08

What it looks for

It answers at three levels of detail, and only goes as deep as the picture supports.

Level 1 — is anything there?

Animal, person, vehicle, or just movement. Always answered.

Level 2 — what kind?

Rodent, bird, reptile, cat/dog, insect. Reliable once the animal is about 40 dots across.

Level 3 — which species?

Rat or mouse, gecko or snake. Only when the animal is big and clear in frame — otherwise it stays quiet rather than guessing.

Why it also detects people

People are not pests, but the class earns its place four times over: it stops human shadows causing false alarms, it tells us whether activity happened during working hours, it lets faces be blurred automatically before any clip leaves the building, and it can prove a technician actually visited at 02:40.

Insects — the honest version

Flying insects cannot be reliably counted by a room camera. Close to the lens they are a huge blur; further away they are smaller than a single dot. There is no camera position that fixes this.

So we do two things that do work: report an activity level (is insect pressure going up in this area this week?) rather than a fake count, and point a close-up camera at the sticky board inside an insect light trap, where the insects are still, flat and evenly lit — and can genuinely be counted. That is also already the measurement the pest-control industry uses.

09

What it works out

One sighting is a fact. Thousands of sightings become answers.

QuestionHow it is answered
How many?Counted after merging sightings of the same animal across cameras.
Getting worse or better?Compared against this site's own normal level, not a generic one.
What time of night?All sightings placed on a 24-hour clock, per species and per area.
Which way did it go?The direction from where the trail started to where it ended.
Where is worst?Sightings per area, adjusted for how much of that area is covered.
Coming back to the same place?Groups sightings by area and time of night across many days. The same spot at the same hour on six nights is a pattern.
Where are they getting in?Collects the spot where each trail first appears. Over weeks these cluster, and the cluster is usually the gap.
Does weather affect it?Sightings matched against temperature and door-open records from the sensors.

One safeguard worth knowing about. If a camera is broken for three nights it records zero sightings, and a naive report would show that as "no pest activity" — the most dangerous wrong answer possible in a food plant. So every count is divided by how long the camera was actually working, and any report covering a period with poor coverage says so on its front page.

From findings to advice

The reports end with specific recommendations, produced by rules written with Express Pesticides' own technicians — not invented by the software. For example:

"Rodents first appeared at the same point near the dock shutter on 11 of the last 14 nights, mostly between 01:40 and 03:10. Inspect the shutter seal and brush strip. Place snap traps along the east wall junction at 3 m spacing. Re-check in 14 nights."

Every recommendation comes with the clips that triggered it, so a technician can judge it rather than take it on faith.

10

When it tells you

Three kinds of alert: something just happened, this keeps happening in the same place, and this area is much busier than normal.

The hard part is not producing alerts. It is producing few enough that people still read them.

Five things that keep the volume sane

  • Only confident ones interrupt people. Uncertain sightings appear in the review list without notifying anyone.
  • Cooling-off period. One rat crossing a doorway six times is one alert, not six.
  • Automatic digest. Past a certain rate the channel switches to an hourly summary and says so. A real infestation should not produce a hundred notifications.
  • Right person, right time. The night supervisor gets dock alerts at 3 a.m.; the account manager gets the morning summary.
  • Escalation. An unacknowledged urgent alert goes to SMS, then to a phone call after 30 minutes.

The target that matters: fewer than one false alarm per camera every two nights. On a sixteen-camera site that is at most eight a night. Above that, clients mute the notifications and the system may as well not exist — so this number, not raw accuracy, is what the project is measured against.

11

Proof and privacy

What gets saved as proof

  • A video clip that starts 8 seconds before the animal appeared and ends 5 seconds after it left
  • A photo with the animal outlined and its path drawn on
  • Site, camera, area, date and time, species, and how confident the system was
  • A digital fingerprint calculated the moment the clip is made, so it can be shown the file was never altered

The outline is stored alongside the video rather than burned into it, and the clean original is kept. A box drawn onto a video is a claim the client cannot check; a clean clip plus separately recorded markings is proof they can. This matters for food-safety audits.

Privacy

Overnight footage in hospitals, hotels and homes contains people. So:

  • Faces are blurred automatically on any clip before it leaves the building
  • Clips are deleted after 90 days by default; the summary data is kept for two years
  • Access is controlled and logged — who watched which clip, and when
  • Nothing has to leave the site at all if a client forbids it. The same software runs entirely on their own server.

This is designed around India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, and is worth a short legal review before the first hospital installation.

12

How it gets better

The recogniser is not fixed at installation. It improves at each site over time, and the mechanism is deliberately simple.

Every sighting in the review screen has three buttons: yes that was a rat, no it was not, or it was actually a bird. That single control is the entire teaching pipeline.

  • Every answer becomes a labelled example.
  • The "no" answers are the most valuable of all — they teach the system what to ignore, which is where most of the difficulty lives.
  • Once a month the recogniser is retrained on everything gathered, tested against a set of nights it has never seen, and only then rolled out.
  • Each site also gets its own sensitivity setting, tuned after a couple of weeks of real sightings. Sites differ enormously; one universal setting is wrong nearly everywhere.

Why the review screen shipped before the fancy dashboard: without it the system is frozen at whatever accuracy it launched with. With it, the system gets measurably better every month it runs, at every site it runs at.

13

What it runs on

The computer inside the building is small, silent and has no moving parts. Size depends on how many cameras it handles.

Site sizeCamerasComputerTypical building
Small2–6Jetson Orin NanoRestaurant, clinic, small residential block
Medium8–16Jetson Orin NXWarehouse, hotel, small factory
Large24–482–3 units, or a small server with a graphics cardFood plant, large logistics hub

For large sites we prefer several small computers to one big one: it is cheaper per camera, and if one fails you lose a third of the site rather than all of it.

The server side starts deliberately small — a single machine handles the first several dozen sites, because all the heavy work already happened in the buildings.

How much data moves

Each sighting sends a tiny message (about 2 KB) and a video clip (about 4 MB). A busy site sends roughly 800 MB overnight — fine on normal broadband, and manageable on mobile data. On a poor connection the system can send the messages immediately and the clips during the day.

14

Where we are

Steps 1 to 7 are built, tested and working. Cameras are read, movement is detected, patches are cut, things are followed, false alarms are filtered, proof is saved, and there is a screen for reviewing it all.

One piece is missing: the recogniser has not been taught yet.

It has to be taught on the kind of pictures it will actually see, and no collection exists of rats in Indian warehouses at 2 a.m. on night-vision CCTV. Nobody has ever made one. So we need a few nights of real footage from two or three sites, and everything else is ready and waiting for it.

Until then the system runs deliberately over-eagerly: it flags anything that moves, so that the moment footage arrives there are plenty of examples to sort through and learn from.

In one sentence: the difficult engineering is finished, and what remains is teaching — which needs footage from the client, not more code.
