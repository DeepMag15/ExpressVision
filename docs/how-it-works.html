How the pest camera system works

No technical background needed. Six short sections and four pictures explaining what we are building and why it is put together this way.

This page is for understanding only. It is not part of the software, and nothing depends on it.
01

The problem

Rats and mice come out at night, when nobody is in the building. Most warehouses, factories and food plants already have cameras recording all night long.

But nobody watches that footage. One camera records about eight hours a night. A site with sixteen cameras produces 128 hours of video every night. Watching it is impossible.

So the recording exists, the rats are on it, and nobody finds out until someone spots droppings weeks later. By then the problem is much bigger and much more expensive.

Our software watches the footage instead. It never gets bored and never sleeps.

02

The one fact that shapes everything

Here is the thing that makes this hard, and it is not what people expect.

In one night, a single camera records roughly 650,000 individual pictures. Of those, maybe 20 have a rat in them. Everything else is an empty floor.

Why this matters more than it sounds: it would be easy to build something that finds every rat — just alert on everything that moves. It would also be easy to build something that never sends a false alarm — just never alert. The whole difficulty is doing both at once.

03

How it works, in five steps

Video comes in one end. Alerts and reports come out the other. In between, five steps, each one throwing away work the next step would waste time on.

1

Look for movement — cheaply

First we just ask: did anything in this picture change since the last one? That is a quick, simple check.

Like a night guard who dozes with one eye open and only properly looks up when something stirs. About 96% of pictures are thrown away here, and they cost almost nothing to check.

2

Zoom in on the moving bit

Something moved in the corner. We cut out just that corner at full quality — rather than shrinking the whole picture down.

This is the most important trick in the whole system. Section 4 explains why.

3

Ask "what is that?"

Now the expensive part runs, but only on the small cut-out piece. It answers: rat, cat, bird, person, or nothing interesting.

Because steps 1 and 2 did the filtering, this runs about 26 times less often than it otherwise would. That is what lets one small computer handle sixteen cameras.

4

Watch it for a few seconds before deciding

We never raise an alarm from one picture. We follow the thing across many pictures and ask: did it actually go anywhere? Did it move like a living creature?

A plant swaying in a draught moves constantly but never gets anywhere. A rat crosses the room. One photo cannot tell them apart; a few seconds of movement can. This step alone removes about 93% of the remaining false alarms.

5

Save the proof and tell someone

Now we are confident. Save a video clip, a still picture with the animal marked, the time, the camera, and the path it walked. Then send the alert.

The clip starts before the rat appeared, because we keep the last few seconds of every camera in memory at all times. Otherwise you only ever see the animal already mid-screen.

04

Why we zoom instead of shrink

This is the one technical idea worth understanding, because everything else depends on it.

Software that recognises objects needs pictures at a fixed small size — think of a passport photo. So the normal approach is to shrink the whole camera picture down to fit.

That works fine for people and cars, because they are big. It destroys rats. Shrinking a wide camera picture makes everything about three times smaller, and a rat that was already tiny becomes a smudge of a dozen dots.

Because step 1 already told us where the movement was, we can do something smarter: cut out that patch at full quality, like using a magnifying glass on one part of a map instead of photocopying the whole map smaller.

There is a limit, and it is set by the camera, not the software. A rat needs to be at least about 40 dots wide before anything can recognise it. Too far from the camera, or too wide a lens, and it simply is not there to be found — no software can invent detail the camera never captured.

This is why the first job at any new site is checking whether its existing cameras can actually see a rat. Some will not, and it is much cheaper to find that out in week one than in month six.

05

What comes out

When the system is sure, one sighting turns into several useful things at once.

Over weeks, all of this adds up into reports: which areas are worst, what time of night they are active, whether the problem is growing or shrinking, and where to put traps.

06

Where we are now

The machinery is built and tested. One piece is missing, and it is the piece that needs the client.

doneReading video from files and live cameras
doneSpotting movement, ignoring still frames
doneZooming in on the moving patch
doneFollowing things and rejecting false alarms
doneSaving clips, photos and paths
doneA screen for reviewing what it found
waitingTeaching it what a rat looks like on these sites

Why that last one is waiting

Software that recognises animals has to be taught by example, and it has to be taught on the kind of pictures it will actually see. There is no existing collection of photographs of rats in Indian warehouses at two in the morning, filmed on night-vision CCTV. Nobody has ever made one.

So we need real night footage from real sites. A few nights from two or three different kinds of building is enough to start. Everything else is ready and waiting for it.

Until then the system runs in a deliberately over-eager mode: it flags anything that moves, so that when footage arrives we have plenty of examples to sort through and learn from.

In one sentence: the hard engineering is finished, and the remaining work is teaching — which needs footage from the client, not more code.
